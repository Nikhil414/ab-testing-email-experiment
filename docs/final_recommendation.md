# Final case-study recommendation

**Recommendation: use the historical evidence to justify a fresh controlled experiment, rather than an immediate rollout to today's customers.**

In this 64,000-customer randomized experiment, both email campaigns increased purchase conversion compared with no email. Mens E-Mail increased conversion by **0.681 percentage points** (97.5% interval: **0.474 to 0.887**); Womens E-Mail increased it by **0.311 percentage points** (97.5% interval: **0.127 to 0.495**). Both comparisons meet the specified significance criterion after Bonferroni correction for the two planned tests.

Revenue per assigned customer also increased: the estimated differences were **0.7698** for Mens E-Mail and **0.4244** for Womens E-Mail, in source currency units. Their respective 97.5% intervals were **0.4443 to 1.0954** and **0.1323 to 0.7166**. These are revenue estimates, not profit estimates.

Mens E-Mail has the larger observed effect against control, but this analysis does not establish that it outperforms Womens E-Mail. No direct campaign-to-campaign test was specified or performed.

Before acting today, run a new three-arm experiment with current customers. Define a minimum worthwhile improvement, sample size, stopping rule, and any direct campaign comparison in advance. Collect campaign costs, contribution margins, cancellations, and unsubscribe/complaint outcomes so the decision considers profitability and customer harm.

The original data comes from 2008. Missing customer identifiers and assignment logs prevent independent identity and contamination checks; repeated records were retained. Revenue intervals use large-sample approximations. These limitations qualify the recommendation.
